A seven-day study plan that actually fits in a week

128 questions sound like a lot. They are not: most answers are one word, and the list is grouped in themes that reinforce each other. With 43 minutes of audio and five scored tests, one week of 25 minutes a day is enough for most people. This is the order we would use.

Day by day

The rule that makes it work

Answer out loud, before the audio gives the answer. If you read the answer first, you are training recognition — the feeling of "yes, I knew that" — which collapses the moment a person asks you. Speaking forces retrieval, which is the only thing the interview measures.

What to do with a question you keep missing

  1. Write the number on a single sheet of paper. Not the answer, the number.
  2. Say the correct answer aloud three times, looking at it.
  3. Cover it and say it again. If it does not come out, repeat — do not move on. A question you "sort of know" is a question you will miss under stress.
  4. Ask someone to test you on your numbers only, in random order, once a day.

Questions worth extra attention

  • The three branches and who does what (16–20, 41–51): the most common place to mix up answers.
  • Numbers (7, 21, 22, 24, 25, 27, 36, 53, 54): 27 amendments, 100 senators, 435 representatives, 9 seats, 5 justices. Learning them as a block makes them stick.
  • Amendments (14th, 15th, 19th, 22nd, 10th): who each one covers, men's vote, women's vote, term limits, states' powers.
  • The eight answers that change: check them the morning of the interview at uscis.gov/citizenship/testupdates.

If you have three days, not seven

Day 1: themes 1, 2 and 3 audio, plus you write the eight personal answers. Day 2: themes 4, 5, 6, 7 audio, plus two practice tests. Day 3: only your missed list, one more test, then stop. It is a worse plan than seven days, but it beats reading 128 questions once and hoping.
