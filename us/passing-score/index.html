How the civics test is scored: 12 out of 20

The 2025 version of the test does not ask all 128 questions. It asks up to 20 and stops early. Understanding the rules is worth as much as memorising: it tells you when you have already won.

Why "up to 20" matters

The officer counts both ways. Reach 12 correct and the test ends in your favour — you may be asked only 15 questions. Reach 9 incorrect first and it ends against you. In practice this means a bad start is survivable: if you miss the first three and then answer fifteen correctly, you pass. Panic is a bigger risk than a gap in knowledge, which is exactly why practising aloud — with a voice that keeps going after a miss — is worth more than a fourth read of the list.

The 60 percent rule

USCIS keeps the same 60 percent standard as the previous 100-question test. 12/20 is 60 percent. There is no partial credit, no half point for a nearly-right answer, and no possibility of bargaining: the officer applies the list of acceptable answers.

Worded differently, still correct

Answers do not have to be verbatim. The USCIS list gives several acceptable answers per question precisely because people phrase them differently, and USCIS says applicants are encouraged to use the published answers but that other correct answers exist. "Six years" and "a senator serves six years" are the same answer. What fails is a different fact: confusing the Senate with the House, or saying "the president writes the laws".

If you fail

You have a second opportunity: the re-examination, normally 60 to 90 days after the first interview. Use it to work only on the questions you missed. Two failures in total mean the application is denied and a new application and fee are required, so the second attempt deserves the same preparation as the first.
